<h2><span class="num">01</span>What you've heard</h2>

<div class="heard">"AI is draining our water. Every chatbot question 'drinks' a bottle of water, and data centers are sucking communities dry."</div>

You have probably seen the memes: a data center as a giant straw in a dry lakebed. The worry is real enough to take seriously. It is also, at the national level, badly out of proportion.

<h2><span class="num">02</span>What's actually true</h2>

Start with the comparison that ends most arguments.

<Stat
  big="≈ 2–3×"
  desc="U.S. golf courses applied about 531 billion gallons of water in 2024. All U.S. data centers used about 229 billion gallons in 2023, counting the water used to generate their electricity (about 17 billion on site, about 211 billion at power plants). Older estimates put data centers lower, about 163 billion, which is where the often-quoted 3× comes from."
  source="GCSAA golf survey (Dec 2025, our gallon conversion); Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, 2024 Data Center Energy Usage Report (our liter-to-gallon conversion)"
/>

Zoom out and data centers nearly vanish. The United States withdrew about 117 trillion gallons of water in 2015, the latest national total from the U.S. Geological Survey.<sup><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup> All of its data centers combined, even counting the water behind their electricity, come to about two-tenths of one percent of that.<sup><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup>

<Stat
  big="117 trillion gal"
  desc="Total U.S. water withdrawn in 2015. All U.S. data centers combined used about 229 billion gallons in 2023, roughly 0.2% of that."
  source="Ars Technica (2026), citing USGS; Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (2024)"
/>

Plenty of ordinary things use far more. U.S. lawns and landscaping drink about 3.3 trillion gallons a year. California's almond orchards use about 1.3 trillion. Each of those alone dwarfs every data center in the country.<sup><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup>

<Stat
  big="Lawns 3.3T · Almonds 1.3T"
  desc="U.S. lawns and landscaping, and California almond orchards, each use far more water per year than all U.S. data centers (about 229 billion gallons)."
  source="Ars Technica (2026)"
/>

Even the biggest individual companies are small against the national total, though they are growing fast. Amazon reported its data centers withdrew about 2.5 billion gallons worldwide in 2025.<sup><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup> Google's data centers withdrew about 13.6 billion gallons in 2025 and consumed about 10.5 billion, and the company's total water consumption rose by about a third from 2024.<sup><a href="#source-6">6</a></sup>

The "a bottle of water per question" line comes from an estimate by University of California, Riverside scientists of about 519 milliliters for a 100-word prompt.<sup><a href="#source-7">7</a></sup> Companies now publish their own, much smaller numbers. Google says a typical Gemini text prompt uses 0.26 milliliters, about five drops.<sup><a href="#source-6">6</a></sup> OpenAI's Sam Altman says an average ChatGPT query uses about one fifteenth of a teaspoon.<sup><a href="#source-8">8</a></sup> Mistral puts a 400-token answer from its assistant at 45 milliliters.<sup><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup> The answer depends on what you count, and the companies' figures aren't independently checked. None of them adds up to a national crisis.

<h2><span class="num">03</span>Where the real concern is</h2>

Here is the part the memes get right, just for the wrong reason. The honest worry isn't the national total. It's **local**. A data center draws its water from one place, and in the wrong place that draw really bites.

A single Meta data center in Newton County, Georgia uses about 10 percent of the county's water.<sup><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup> In the Washington, D.C. metro area, data centers take only 1 percent of the water withdrawn, but they account for 9 percent of the water used up and not returned, and up to 12 percent in summer, when rivers run lowest. Across the whole Potomac basin it is 3 percent.<sup><a href="#source-4">4</a></sup> Texas data centers consumed about 25 billion gallons in 2025 by the Houston Advanced Research Center's estimate, about 0.4 percent of the state's water use, and that could grow to between 29 and 161 billion gallons by 2030.<sup><a href="#source-5">5</a></sup> And a 2025 Business Insider analysis found about 40 percent of planned and existing U.S. data centers sit in areas of high or extremely high water scarcity.<sup><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup>

That is a real, specific, winnable fight, and a very different one from "AI is an environmental catastrophe."

<div class="real-move">
  <h2><span class="num">04</span>The progressive move</h2>
  <p>Don't hand the water issue over to bad math. Push for the things that actually protect communities: public-interest siting rules, requirements to recycle cooling water and run hotter or air-cooled where possible, and real transparency about how much a facility draws locally. The goal isn't to stop the build. It's to make sure the towns hosting this infrastructure share in the benefits instead of just absorbing the costs.</p>
