<h2><span class="num">01</span>What you've heard</h2>

<div class="heard">"AI is guzzling unsustainable amounts of electricity. Every chatbot question burns ten times a Google search, data centers are about to break the grid, and the whole thing is cooking the planet."</div>

The growth is real, and parts of this are worth worrying about. But the "AI is breaking the grid" headline gets the scale wrong, and points the alarm at the wrong target.

<h2><span class="num">02</span>What's actually true</h2>

Data centers are a real and fast-growing load. They are not, at the national scale, an outlier.

<Stat
  big="4.7%"
  desc="Share of total U.S. electricity used by all data centers in 2024 (about 192 TWh). Berkeley Lab's main projection for 2030 is 11.8%, inside a wide range of 9.5% to 15.3%, not a settled number."
  source="Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, United States Data Center Energy Usage Report: 2025 Update (June 2026)"
/>

Here is a comparison that puts it in scale. In 2024, every data center in the country combined used less electricity than Americans running their home air conditioners. That won't last long: Berkeley Lab projects data centers will use about 649 TWh by 2030, far more than home air conditioning, so the comparison is likely to flip within a few years.<sup><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup>

<Stat
  big="Data centers < home A/C (for now)"
  desc="All U.S. data centers used about 192 TWh of electricity in 2024. U.S. residential air conditioning alone used about 254 TWh (2020, the latest detailed federal estimate). Data center use is growing fast and is likely to pass it soon."
  source="U.S. Energy Information Administration; Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (2026)"
/>

Worldwide it's a smaller slice still: data centers use just over 1% of global electricity.<sup><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup> And while that number is climbing, data centers are only about 8% of the *growth* in global electricity demand expected this decade, behind both electric vehicles and air conditioning.<sup><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup>

<Stat
  big="~8% of demand growth"
  desc="Data centers account for roughly 8% of the projected growth in global electricity demand through 2030, less than electric vehicles or air conditioning."
  source="Carbon Brief, citing the IEA's Energy and AI report (2025)"
/>

The per-question scare doesn't hold up either. A typical ChatGPT query uses about 0.3 watt-hours, roughly what an LED bulb or a laptop draws in a few minutes, not the viral figure that was an order of magnitude higher.<sup><a href="#source-4">4</a></sup>

<h2><span class="num">03</span>Where the real concern is</h2>

Here's what the "grid apocalypse" framing buries: the legitimate fight isn't about the national total. It's about **who pays for the buildout, and what gets burned to power it.** Both are going the wrong way, and both are fixable.

On cost, PJM, the grid operator for 67 million people across the mid-Atlantic and Midwest,<sup><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup> ran another record-setting capacity auction in December 2025, and its own independent market monitor found data center demand drove about 40% of the cost.<sup><a href="#source-5">5</a></sup> Its next auction, in July 2026, again cleared at the price cap and came up 6,831 megawatts short of its reliability target.<sup><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup>

<Stat
  big="40% of $16.4B"
  desc="Data center load accounted for about 40% of the costs in PJM's record December 2025 capacity auction."
  source="PJM's Independent Market Monitor (Monitoring Analytics), via Utility Dive"
/>

Researchers at Harvard's Electricity Law Initiative found ways utilities shift the cost of serving data centers onto everyday ratepayers, especially confidential special contracts that give tech companies discounted rates. One study of data centers in Virginia found ratepayers there could see their electricity costs rise by $150 to $450 a year by 2040.<sup><a href="#source-6">6</a></sup>

And what powers all of it is sliding backward. As of December 2025, utilities had delayed the retirement of at least 15 coal plants that year, mostly to meet expected demand growth driven largely by data centers.<sup><a href="#source-7">7</a></sup> In Memphis, residents and advocates say xAI ran dozens of gas turbines near a predominantly Black neighborhood with high asthma rates before it had air permits.<sup><a href="#source-8">8</a></sup> In March 2026, Mississippi regulators approved 41 permitted gas turbines at xAI's plant across the state line in Southaven, replacing 27 unpermitted ones, over local opposition.<sup><a href="#source-10">10</a></sup> That is the real cost, and it lands on specific communities.

<div class="real-move">
  <h2><span class="num">04</span>The progressive move</h2>
  <p>Don't fight the data center. Fight the bad deal. Make Big Tech, not families, pay for the grid upgrades its facilities require, through cost-causation rate design and the kind of rules Virginia and federal regulators are now testing. Tie the buildout to clean firm power; the wave of nuclear and geothermal deals shows it can be done, and push for around-the-clock clean matching instead of paper credits. And block the fossil lock-in, especially where it dumps pollution on the communities least able to fight back. The grid can handle AI. The question is who pays for it, and whether it runs clean.</p>
</div>
