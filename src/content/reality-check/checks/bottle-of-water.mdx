<h2><span class="num">02</span>Where the claim came from</h2>

In September 2024 The Washington Post worked with researchers at the University of California, Riverside to estimate how much water and electricity ChatGPT, running GPT-4, uses to write a 100-word email.<sup><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup> The method builds on the team's 2023 preprint, "Making AI Less Thirsty."<sup><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup> The bottle comparison spread from there.

<h2><span class="num">05</span>What the headline gets right</h2>

AI does use water, mostly to cool servers and to generate the electricity they run on. The Riverside team's work was among the first to make that visible, and companies have published more of their own figures since.<sup><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup><sup><a href="#source-4">4</a></sup>

<h2><span class="num">06</span>What it gets wrong or leaves out</h2>

"A bottle per email" reads like a fixed fact. It is a modeled estimate for one model in 2024, and it changes several-fold depending on the data center's location and power source. It also invites people to scale a per-use number into a national crisis, when all U.S. data centers together use a sliver of the country's water.<sup><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup>

<h2><span class="num">07</span>Where the real concern is</h2>

The honest worry is local. A data center draws from one watershed, and roughly 40 percent of planned and existing U.S. data centers sit in areas of high or extremely high water scarcity.<sup><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup> That is where siting rules and disclosure matter.

<h2><span class="num">08</span>What we're not sure about</h2>

Per-prompt water use for today's models is mostly self-reported by the companies, with different methods. Independent, current measurements would settle it.

<div class="real-move">
  <h2><span class="num">09</span>The progressive move</h2>
  <p>Push for mandatory, facility-level water reporting and siting rules for water-stressed areas, rather than arguing over per-email math.</p>
</div>
